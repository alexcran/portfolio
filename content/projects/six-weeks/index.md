## Six Weeks

*As he retires this week, Todd looks back on more than forty years in dairy and a career he never set out to build.*

The milk truck pulled up to the back dock the way it always did, and Todd, a teenager working in his father's grocery store in Washington's Yakima Valley, went out to help. He checked the cottage cheese against the order, stacked the milk crates, and put the product away while the driver counted. Later, the driver asked Todd's father a question: Would the kid be willing to work at the dairy? A man on the back dock had broken his leg, and the plant needed someone to fill in for six weeks. Todd said yes.

That kid had no idea he was saying yes to a career that would last more than forty years.

The work was physical. He unloaded milk crates and plastic cases, loaded trucks, and moved product wherever it needed to go. The plant was small enough that the jobs ran together, and over the years he worked nearly all of them, from production and the fillers to the lab to butter and ice cream mix. He wasn't trying to climb any career ladder or move up. He was trying to learn. "I was never trying to get to the next rung," he said. "It was more: 'what else can I learn?'"

That instinct led him to a decision that looked, on paper, like a step backward: a lab manager job opened at another plant. It meant leaving a stable, reliable job for a non-union role that paid less and was far from home. People told him not to take it, but he did it anyway. "That was the best thing I ever did," he said. He learned more and grew. From there the moves came steadily, across plants and cities all over the Northwest, and by his early thirties he was running his first plant. He never drew up a plan for any of it. "I never wanted to take a step up," he said. "I just wanted to learn and get to the next square on the checkerboard."

He carried that first plant floor with him into every job that followed. By the time he was leading plants and eventually Maola's operations, his role had changed but his connection to the plant floor hadn't. Because he had done the physical labor himself, running the lines and making the mix, he understood what those jobs asked of people and it changed the way he talked to them. "You know what their life's like," he said. "You're able to relate to them, speak the same language."

By the time he reached the senior ranks of operations, he faced an inevitable choice: stay with what he knew or bet on something new. He had been with his company a long time, and he could have stayed comfortably. The real question, as he put it, was whether he was going to keep doing the same thing for the rest of his life or take a risk and push himself toward something more. "As soon as I said it like that, it just clicked," he said.

Todd came to Maola a little over eleven years ago. What struck him wasn't just the work, which runs on familiar rhythms across the industry, but the people, who were focused on the job in front of them rather than on how it was perceived or looked. "Just good, solid people," he said. "Just real people doing real work." He would rise to Executive Vice President of Operations, the role he leaves at the end of this week.

Even though he was traveling east to come work at Maola, when he arrived it felt a little closer to the wild west. He had come up in an environment built on metrics and continuous improvement, on always reaching for the next thing, and Maola measured success more simply. "If we got the milk run that day, it was a good day," he said, and often little beyond that was tracked at all. On his first tours of the plants, he was handed the financials. He wanted to know about the operation. Were they watching their moisture? Their butterfat? More than once the questions drew blank looks, as though no one was quite sure where he had come from.

So he started building, slowly. He sat with the controllers and worked up metrics, and at first, not everyone understood what he was after. He kept at it. He never handed anyone a finished system and told them to run it; he tried to give people the pieces and let each plant work out what fit.

That is the heart of what he believes. The things that last are never the programs but the habits, the ones repeated until no one bothers to name them anymore. He has watched companies seize on the next big methodology, announce it loudly, and forget it within a year. "What you make and what you start has to be something you do every day," he said. "You've got to make it who you are." The proof, for him, is in the ordinary moments now. Sit in on a plant review or one of the nine-o'clock calls today and you will hear people talk not only about what they did, but about the numbers behind it and how they got there. That language wasn't always part of the conversation. When it shows up on its own, with no one prompting it, he knows it stuck.

Ask him what he will miss, and the answer comes before the question is finished. "People," he said. He means the employees who show up and do the work, the farmers whose milk moves through the cooperative, and the hands that carry it the rest of the way. He is quick to add that whatever changed on his watch was never his alone. The improvements at the plants and the culture that grew up around them belong to the people who were already doing the work and chose to make it their own. "I just kind of helped keep it going," he said.

Ask him what that kid on the back dock would make of all this, the one checking cottage cheese against an order with no idea what he wanted to do, and Todd goes quiet. "I couldn't even conceptualize," he said. He managed to turn a six-week temp job into more than a forty-year career. It started on a back dock and carried him all the way to leading the work he once did himself.
